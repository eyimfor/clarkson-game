Think first
Where does the 5,500 come from, and is it reasonable?

STEP 4 OF 16
Calculate 1996 COGS

Formula:

COGS = Sales × (COGS/Sales ratio)

Given:

  • 1996 Sales = $5,500
  • COGS/Sales ratio = 75.0%
Think first
COGS/Sales was 75.4%, 75.8% and 75.8% in 1993-95. Why does the forecast use 75.0% in 1996 and 74.0% after?

The new credit line lets Clarkson pay suppliers within 10 days and take the 2% trade discount. Purchases are about 79% of sales (3,579 / 4,519 in 1995), so 2% off purchases is worth 0.02 × 0.79 = 1.6 points of sales. The historical 75.6% less 1.6 points is 74.0%, the case figure from 1997 on. The 75.0% in 1996 is a partial year: the credit line arrives "in a few weeks."

Notice that the margin ratios barely moved across the three years. For margins, "use 1995" and "use the average" give the same answer. That is not true of the working-capital ratios, as you will see at the receivables step.

This ratio is also the one that matters most. One percentage point of COGS/Sales, held for every year, moves Clarkson's enterprise value by about a third. One point of the discount rate moves it by about a sixth.

STEP 10 OF 16
Calculate 1996 Accounts Receivable

Formula:

AR = (AR Days on Hand) × (Sales / 365)

Given:

  • AR Days on Hand = 43.4 days
  • 1996 Sales = $5,500
Think first
AR days were 48.9 in 1995. The case uses 43.4, the 1993-95 average. Which would you use?
199319941995Average
AR days (AR / daily sales)38.243.148.943.4

The case for 48.9: it is the most recent number and reflects how the business is run today. If collection periods are genuinely drifting up, the average is biased low and gets more wrong every year.

The case for 43.4: one year is noisy, and 1995 was unusual (the Holtz buyout drained cash). More important, days on hand divides a year-end balance by a full-year flow. A firm growing 30% a year has December sales running about 14% above its annual average, so year-end receivables look long even when customers pay exactly as before. Measured against the December run rate, 1995 AR days are about 42.9, essentially the average.

The choice is not cosmetic. Using 1995's working-capital ratios instead of the averages lowers Clarkson's equity value by roughly a fifth, because every extra day of receivables and inventory is cash tied up permanently in a growing business.

STEP 12 OF 16
Calculate 1996 Accounts Payable

First, calculate Purchases:

Purchases = ΔInventory + COGS
ΔInventory = Inventory(1996) - Inventory(1995)

Then calculate AP:

AP = (AP Days on Hand) × (Purchases / 365)

Given:

  • Inventory(1995) = $587 (Exhibit 2, "Inventory", 1995)
  • Inventory(1996) = $671
  • 1996 COGS = $4,125
  • AP Days on Hand = 10 days
Think first
Historical AP days were 35, 46 and 38 (average 39.7). Why does the forecast throw that history away and use 10?

Because the policy changes. Suppliers offer 2/10 net 30: a 2% discount for paying within 10 days, otherwise the full amount in 30. Clarkson has been paying at 40 to 45 days because he had no cash to do otherwise. The Northrup credit line lets him pay in 10 days and take the discount, so the forecast uses 10. History is where a forecast starts; when you know something is about to change, you override it.

Forgoing the discount is expensive borrowing from suppliers: giving up 2% to pay 20 days later costs (2 / 98) × (365 / 20), about 37% a year on the stated terms, or about 21% at the 45 days Clarkson actually takes. The bank lends at about 11%.

Paying earlier costs cash up front. Moving AP from 45 to 10 days ties up roughly 400 in 1996, which is why 1996 free cash flow is so negative, and why the business cannot do this without the new loan.

Also notice why AP is driven by purchases rather than COGS: suppliers are owed for what was bought. When inventory is growing, purchases exceed COGS (4,209 against 4,125 in 1996).

The complete DCF valuation

What you've mastered is Step 1 of the complete DCF process. Here's the full roadmap:


What you completed

  1. Project 1996 Free Cash Flow (All components: Sales, COGS, OpEx, EBIT, NOP, ΔNFA, ΔNOWC, FCF)

Next steps in a complete DCF

  1. Repeat for years 1997-2000: Use the same process you just learned to project FCF for each year using the ratios in the case
  2. Discount all FCFs to present value: Use the case's discount rate of 11.5% to discount each year's FCF back to the end of 1995
  3. Estimate the residual value (RV): After 2000, sales grow at a steady 5% a year, so use the growing perpetuity: RV₂₀₀₀ = FCF₂₀₀₁ / (r - g)
  4. Discount the RV: Bring RV₂₀₀₀ back to the end of 1995
  5. Sum to get Enterprise Value: EV = PV(FCF 1996-2000) + PV(RV)
  6. Calculate Market Value of Equity: MV Equity = EV + Non-Operating Assets - Debt

Last link in the chain

Once you have Clarkson's enterprise value from the roadmap above, the equity value uses your answers to Steps 1 and 2 of this game:

MV Equity = EV + Non-Op Assets - Debt

Final check: Enter the enterprise value you computed for Clarkson, then the market value of equity it implies (both in thousands).

What the ratios are worth

Once you complete the full valuation, try changing one assumption at a time and watch enterprise value. Roughly:

One-notch changeEffect on enterprise value
COGS/Sales up 1 percentage point, every yearabout −36%
Operating expenses/Sales up 1 point, every yearabout −35%
Discount rate 11.5% to 12.5%about −18%
Terminal growth 5% to 4%about −6%
Net fixed asset turnover 12.5 to 11.5about −4%
AR days up 1 dayabout −1.5%

The residual value is more than 100% of enterprise value (the 1996-2000 cash flows sum to a small negative present value), so the ratios that hold in 2000 are the valuation. Steady-state margins matter most; the discount rate matters about half as much; working-capital days matter least. Most people guess the opposite order.
